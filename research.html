HNRNPA2B1


HNRNPA2B1 (Heterogeneous Nuclear Ribonucleoprotein A2/B1) is a multifunctional RNA-binding protein that acts as a crucial master regulator of gene expression, primarily involved in RNA splicing, trafficking, stability, and translation. As part of the hnRNP family, it is essential for processing nascent pre-mRNA in the nucleus and shuttling between the nucleus and cytoplasm to regulate mRNA metabolism. HNRNPA2B1 is notably recognized as an "reader," binding to N6-methyladenosine-modified transcripts to promote primary microRNA processing and alternative splicing. The gene generates two major alternatively spliced isoforms, A2 and B1, which are highly conserved and play significant roles in telomere maintenance, cell proliferation, and glucose transport.

Aberrant expression or mutation of HNRNPA2B1 is heavily implicated in various diseases, including cancers (such as lung, pancreatic, and liver cancer), where it often promotes a pro-proliferative and anti-apoptotic phenotype. In cancer cells, it is frequently upregulated, aiding tumor growth, metastasis, and therapy resistance by stabilizing oncogenic mRNAs and modulating signaling pathways like PI3K/Akt and Wnt/ -catenin. Additionally, mutations in this protein have been linked to neurodegenerative disorders like ALS and dementia, as well as muscular dystrophy, often through the formation of pathological aggregates. Due to its critical role in promoting cancer progression, HNRNPA2B1 is a promising diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target, with studies showing that its inhibition can rescue disease phenotypes in pulmonary arterial hypertension and cancer models.

PARP


Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) is a family of 17 nuclear enzymes that play a fundamental role in maintaining genomic integrity by identifying and signaling DNA damage. PARP1, the most abundant family member, acts as a molecular "nick sensor" that rapidly binds to single-strand breaks (SSBs). Once bound to a DNA lesion, the enzyme uses nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide ( ) as a substrate to catalyze the formation of poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) chains on itself and target proteins like histones. This biochemical process, known as PARylation, serves as a scaffold to recruit essential repair proteins—including XRCC1, DNA polymerase , and DNA ligase III—to the site of damage, facilitating efficient base excision repair (BER).

In clinical oncology, PARP has gained significant importance as a therapeutic target, particularly through the use of PARP inhibitors like Olaparib and Niraparib. These drugs work by blocking the catalytic activity of PARP and "trapping" the enzyme on DNA, which prevents the repair of SSBs and leads to the formation of lethal double-strand breaks (DSBs) during DNA replication. This mechanism exploits "synthetic lethality," specifically killing cancer cells that already have defective homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathways, such as those with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations. Beyond cancer, excessive PARP activation can lead to cellular energy failure through the depletion of and ATP, contributing to the pathology of inflammatory and neurodegenerative conditions.

Immunotherapy


Immune therapy has fundamentally changed the treatment of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), a subtype that lacks targetable receptors and was previously reliant almost entirely on chemotherapy. TNBC is uniquely suited for immunotherapy because it often has higher levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), a greater tumor mutational burden (TMB), and more frequent expression of the PD-L1 protein compared to other breast cancers. Currently, the primary strategy involves immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), specifically the PD-1 inhibitor pembrolizumab (Keytruda). In early-stage disease, pembrolizumab is given alongside neoadjuvant chemotherapy to increase the likelihood of a pathological complete response (pCR), while in the metastatic setting, it is used for patients whose tumors test positive for PD-L1.

Current research is focused on overcoming resistance in "cold" tumors—those that lack sufficient immune activity to respond to standard checkpoint blockade. Emerging clinical trials are testing powerful combination strategies, such as pairing ICIs with antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) like sacituzumab govitecan, or PARP inhibitors for patients with BRCA mutations. Other innovative approaches under investigation include CAR-T cell therapy and oncolytic viruses, which aim to directly lyse cancer cells and "prime" the immune system to recognize them as foreign. These advancements are moving the field toward personalized immunotherapy protocols that use deep biomarker profiling to tailor treatments to each patient's specific tumor microenvironment
